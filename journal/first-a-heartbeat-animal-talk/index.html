Field notes / SOFI TUKKER / October 27, 2026

First, a heartbeat.

A creation story, Mary Oliver’s Wild Geese, and what SOFI TUKKER’s world can teach us about taste in AI and company events.

Words & work by Chloe Starr

First, light. Then a heartbeat. Then creatures finding one another through sound.

Chris and I were at SOFI TUKKER’s Animal Talk at Cow Palace on Saturday. In the long opening clip I brought home, a creation story unfolds across the stage screens. Animal silhouettes move inside an illuminated shape. The words beside them lead from a world taking form to creatures gathering around rhythm.

The screen gives the story its simplest terms: “…there was only sound.” Later: “…they united.” Sound becomes a way to find one another. That is the spine of this night, and of the field film made from it.

Mary Oliver’s Wild Geese is the other text to keep beside that story. Sophie has identified it as an inspiration for their early work and for the Animal Talk world. The opening creation story is its own show narration; I am keeping the two texts distinct.

Wild Geese · Mary Oliver · Short excerpt

You only have to let the soft animal of your body
love what it loves.
Mary Oliver, Wild Geese, from Dream Work (1986).
Hear Oliver read the complete poem at On Being.

The poem loosens the demand to justify yourself, makes room for grief, and turns outward toward a world that continues around you. Its final movement is toward belonging. That is a useful shape for a room full of people: arrive carrying whatever you brought, then find yourself among other bodies.

A poem can already be a room

Poetry had entered their dance space before Animal Talk. Their own January 22, 2021 livestream, uploaded five days later, pairs their DJ set with Amanda Gorman’s inauguration poem. The published tracklist places her words alongside Jayda G’s “Both of Us” and a remix of York’s “On the Beach.” That is a specific creative choice: a poem can change what people are gathering around without stopping them from dancing.

The other reference I sent over was Rick Rubin’s The Creative Act: A Way of Being. In the publisher’s excerpt, “The Vessel and the Filter”, perception selects from incoming experience, and new material forms relationships with what is already there. I want that idea to do something in the film. The poem, the livestream, the stage and our photographs need to change one another when they meet.

Then the room becomes physical again

There is a line from those separate homes to a room where people can stand beside one another. I would not assume everyone at Cow Palace belonged to the livestream community. But knowing that history changes the scale of what SOFI TUKKER have been making. The invitation has had to survive different kinds of rooms.

I write poems and make films. Here, a creation story gave the show a beginning, and a poem helped explain the invitation behind it. The animals, the stage and the crowd could belong to one unfolding thought. For my own work, that is a concrete question to take home: how can words establish a world that the image, sound and people then inhabit?

Datamoshing belongs inside that question. The companion film carries an earlier livestream image into the movement of the 2026 concert, then restores a clean image before returning to a person. That is the job I gave the effect: loosen the boundary for a moment, then let us recognize someone.

The film comes back to a photograph of Chris and me, with the room opening out behind us. Someone beside me in a place we went to together.

Datamoshing: the technique used in the film

Avidemux documents the relationship between self-contained frames and predictive frames. Antonio Roberts demonstrates repeating predictive frames to create a bloom. Interfering with a scene’s reset can also carry earlier image information into incoming motion. The field film uses the carryover approach. The concert’s own visual production method has not been identified.

Sources & connections

Further notes, people & sources

The animal was there from the beginning

SOFI TUKKER’s first EP was called Soft Animals. In the Animal Talk announcement reported by EDM.com, Sophie Hawley-Weld traced that title to Oliver and described the early record as a continuing guide to the world she and Tucker Halpern wanted to make. She connected it to bodily instinct and communication that can happen before language.

That history changes how the stage reads. In my photograph, Sophie stands beside a microphone that curves like a snake. She and Tucker occupy separate round platforms edged in turquoise. Smoke crosses the space between them. Through the poem, the set reads as a place for creatures to gather.

Other coverage makes the connection explicit. A review of the Chicago Animal Talk show describes animal cutouts accompanying Oliver’s poem in the opening visuals, with its ideas returning through the performance. That is another night’s account, supporting the tour’s larger design. The Cow Palace opening is the one I attended.

Two different ways into a song

Both graduated from Brown University in 2014. Sophie’s Ivy League education has a specific musical afterlife. In Brown Alumni Magazine, she credited Portuguese classes with Patricia Sobral as an influence on her songwriting. Music professor Joshua Tucker’s university CV also records her 2012 independent study in music research and performance in Rio de Janeiro. Her relationship with Brazilian music involved study, language and time in the place.

In a 2018 interview with Bustle, Sophie described studying development, taking dance and literature classes, and continuing to make music in Portuguese after leaving Brazil because she wanted to keep that connection alive. Tucker had gone to Brown for architecture and basketball. Illness interrupted the athletic future he had expected; learning production on his computer opened a different one.

The meeting that became SOFI TUKKER happened at a Brown art event. Sophie was performing original bossa nova in Portuguese. Tucker was booked to DJ. As they recalled to Brown Alumni Magazine, he heard her, introduced himself, and remixed a song there. You can hear the creative relationship in that beginning: one person brings a musical world, and the other hears a way to move it.

Their personalities deserve more than a neat opposites label. In Rebecca Ausseil’s 2017 NYLON interview, Sophie is discussing her education when Tucker loses his concentration over a passing pug. Sophie calmly recognizes his preferred kind of dog and explains that she likes big ones. It is a tiny scene of attention and familiarity, with room for both people’s enthusiasms.

A 2026 ArtStars conversation adds something less polished. They discuss the pressure Sophie once put on herself to exercise and eat perfectly, and becoming more forgiving about life on tour. Friendship and staying close to other people are part of how they describe sustaining the work. That gives the poem a practical human context.

A room they kept opening

When touring stopped during COVID, the gathering moved into their home. Pollstar’s September 2020 interview gives the modest beginning: Sophie was exercising while Tucker DJed, their photographer started streaming, and they decided to do it again. By that report, they had kept going for more than 180 days.

The routine became the Freak Fam. Pollstar reported fans helping one another with rent and internet access, and more than 500 submitted dance videos for the “House Arrest” video. People were contributing to the gathering as well as watching it.

By TIME’s March 2021 report, the daily sequence had reached 383 days, sometimes with friends guesting. The streams ran at 1 p.m. from Miami across several platforms. Fans built their own Zoom room, Discord and other spaces around them.

In the later ArtStars interview, Tucker explains what talking during those streams made possible: people could encounter their personalities in ways a festival performance did not always allow. Sophie names the intimacy that could form online. Their account makes the work of community concrete: return, speak, recognize one another, return again.

Taste is the world you invite people into

SOFI TUKKER have made a world that can hold a poem, a creation story, a stage full of animals and a daily appointment in someone’s living room. Its continuity comes from choices: which words belong, which sounds move the body, how the performers relate to each other, and what the audience gets to become inside it.

That brings the show back to AI for me. Companies are making these choices too. The artists they invite onto their stages help give a company’s world a sound, a pace and a set of associations. An artist arrives with a practice and a public of their own. Choosing them brings that history into the room.

There is a specific example at Anthropic’s first Code with Claude in 2025. In his firsthand production account, event marketer Adrian Santos describes a warehouse afterparty with an algorave DJ making live music and graphics through code. He also describes attendee-created graphics projected at the party and a poem-generating camera. Those choices put programming into the evening’s cultural form as well as its technical sessions. My reading is that the artist’s method helped the experience belong to the developer gathering.

At a different scale, Salesforce announced Usher and Gwen Stefani for Dreamfest 2026, its benefit concert for UCSF Benioff Children’s Hospitals. That choice sits inside a world of customers, partners, employees, popular music and fundraising. I read that combination as a choice about scale, familiarity and who the gathering is meant to bring together.

Figma’s account of Config 2025 makes the surrounding work visible: a designed visual language carried into signage, inflatables, custom furniture, merchandise and a street party with a music stage. Its designers describe the decisions that made the parts cohere. A world is accumulated in those details.

As AI makes more kinds of production available, I think this judgment becomes more consequential. You still have to choose what belongs beside what. A powerful tool, a beautiful projection and a famous performer can each work on their own. Giving them a shared reason to be in one room is another kind of authorship.

That is what I want to look for in the companies and events I cover: what do their choices ask people to feel, notice and do together? At Animal Talk, the creation story gives me a way to ask it. Sound reaches another creature. A gathering begins.
