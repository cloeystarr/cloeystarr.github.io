Field notes / SOFI TUKKER / October 27, 2026

First, a heartbeat.

What I took from a night at SOFI TUKKER’s Animal Talk: choosing the artist is part of making the event.

Words & work by Chloe Starr

Animal silhouettes moved across the Cow Palace screens. Chris and I were there for SOFI TUKKER’s Animal Talk, and the show opened with a creation story. The words on screen went from “…there was only sound” to “…they united.” I kept thinking about that little sequence afterward. The show gave us a story about creatures finding each other through sound, then gave us the rest of the night to do it ourselves.

A poem can already be a room

Sophie’s microphone curved like a snake. She and Tucker stood on separate round platforms edged in turquoise, with smoke moving between them. Mary Oliver’s Wild Geese gives me another way to look at what I filmed. In the Animal Talk announcement, Sophie Hawley-Weld says the poem gave their first EP, Soft Animals, its name. The Cow Palace creation story is the show’s own narration. Oliver’s poem helps me hear what the band has been inviting people into since that first record.

You only have to let the soft animal of your body
love what it loves.
Mary Oliver, Wild Geese, from Dream Work (1986).
Hear Oliver read the complete poem at On Being.

They’ve made space for poetry on a dance floor before. In their January 2021 livestream, Amanda Gorman’s inauguration poem sits in the tracklist with Jayda G’s “Both of Us” and a remix of York’s “On the Beach.” I like how ordinary that looks written down. The set gives her words a place without asking anyone to stop dancing.

Who belongs on the stage?

I cover AI events, and this is a useful thing to bring back from a concert: booking the artist is part of making the event. A famous name might get people in the door. The more interesting choice is an artist whose way of working gives those people something to do together.

At Anthropic’s first Code with Claude in 2025, producer Adrian Santos describes an afterparty where an algorave DJ wrote code to make music and graphics live. Attendees made graphics that were projected at the party, too. The activity people spent the day talking about became something they could watch, hear and take part in that night. That booking told me more about what the event valued than a big name on a poster would have.

What I tried in the cut

One reason I love Breathless is the way a film can feel close to someone and still be restless. I like the same tension in this cut: the big room in motion, then a photograph of two people close enough to fit in one frame. I had the band’s old livestream, the footage I brought home from Cow Palace, and that photograph of Chris and me. Each came from a different little world. The part I like about making a film is finding out what happens when those worlds meet.

In the cut, datamoshing lets a trace of the livestream stay on screen as the concert starts to move through it. Then the picture goes clean again. I like that the transition is a little unruly. For a second, two rooms show up in the same image.

The film ends up back with Chris and me and the room opening out behind us. A couple of people who came to hear a band. That was enough to belong to it for a night.

How the datamosh works

Avidemux documents self-contained and predictive video frames. Disrupting a cut’s reset can leave some of the old image in place while the next shot’s motion arrives. Antonio Roberts demonstrates a related technique using repeated predictive frames. The carryover is an effect in my cut; I don’t know what the concert used for its visuals.

Further notes, people & sources

The poem and the tour

A review of the Chicago Animal Talk show describes animal cutouts accompanying Oliver’s poem and its ideas returning throughout the performance. That is another night’s account, separate from the Cow Palace opening I filmed.

Two different ways into a song

Both graduated from Brown University in 2014. Sophie’s Ivy League education has a specific musical afterlife. In Brown Alumni Magazine, she credited Portuguese classes with Patricia Sobral as an influence on her songwriting. Music professor Joshua Tucker’s university CV also records her 2012 independent study in music research and performance in Rio de Janeiro. Her relationship with Brazilian music involved study, language and time in the place.

In a 2018 interview with Bustle, Sophie described studying development, taking dance and literature classes, and continuing to make music in Portuguese after leaving Brazil because she wanted to keep that connection alive. Tucker had gone to Brown for architecture and basketball. Illness interrupted the athletic future he had expected; learning production on his computer opened a different one.

The meeting that became SOFI TUKKER happened at a Brown art event. Sophie was performing original bossa nova in Portuguese. Tucker was booked to DJ. As they recalled to Brown Alumni Magazine, he heard her, introduced himself, and remixed a song there. You can hear the creative relationship in that beginning: one person brings a musical world, and the other hears a way to move it.

Their personalities deserve more than a neat opposites label. In Rebecca Ausseil’s 2017 NYLON interview, Sophie is discussing her education when Tucker loses his concentration over a passing pug. Sophie calmly recognizes his preferred kind of dog and explains that she likes big ones. It is a tiny scene of attention and familiarity, with room for both people’s enthusiasms.

A 2026 ArtStars conversation adds something less polished. They discuss the pressure Sophie once put on herself to exercise and eat perfectly, and becoming more forgiving about life on tour. Friendship and staying close to other people are part of how they describe sustaining the work. That gives the poem a practical human context.

A room they kept opening

When touring stopped during COVID, the gathering moved into their home. Pollstar’s September 2020 interview gives the modest beginning: Sophie was exercising while Tucker DJed, their photographer started streaming, and they decided to do it again. By that report, they had kept going for more than 180 days.

The routine became the Freak Fam. Pollstar reported fans helping one another with rent and internet access, and more than 500 submitted dance videos for the “House Arrest” video. People were contributing to the gathering as well as watching it.

By TIME’s March 2021 report, the daily sequence had reached 383 days, sometimes with friends guesting. The streams ran at 1 p.m. from Miami across several platforms. Fans built their own Zoom room, Discord and other spaces around them.

In the later ArtStars interview, Tucker explains what talking during those streams made possible: people could encounter their personalities in ways a festival performance did not always allow. Sophie names the intimacy that could form online. Their account makes the work of community concrete: return, speak, recognize one another, return again.
