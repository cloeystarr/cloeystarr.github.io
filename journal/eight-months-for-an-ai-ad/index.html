Eight months
for an AI ad.

An anti-AI-slop campaign for KERNEL, animated in France over eight months. A field note from the premiere at Foreign Cinema.

By Chloe Starr · At Foreign Cinema, November 18, 2026

KERNEL makes infrastructure for AI agents. For its first ad, it spent eight months working with an animation studio in France.

An anti-AI-slop ad for an AI company. That was the detail I wanted to keep from its premiere at Foreign Cinema.

Catherine Jue, KERNEL's co-founder and CEO, describes an eight-month collaboration, scenes made by hand and a custom score. The film credits Parallel Studios. The product gives agents access to the internet; the campaign gives that invisible work a carefully made little world.

There is a person at a desk, a hand at a keyboard, a small character moving through green interfaces. Later, a patient stands at a reception desk. The animation moves between people doing things and the systems carrying those things along.

A film needs a room.

The fit belonged in the picture too: a yellow jacket with shaggy cream trim, burgundy pants and platform shoes. I wanted an editorial image that kept the whole look. The round green windows at Foreign Cinema make a frame around it.

Chris and Connor Paton, a co-founder of Tembo, bonded over having their clothes bought for them. Connor's girlfriend shops for him at Abercrombie. I buy a lot of Chris's clothes there too.

My camera went into the kitchen too. Trays of small, carefully arranged bites were being carried out. In the courtyard, the projected greens landed on a red wall and returned in the wet ground. A person could watch the screen, or look down and find another version of it.

The film on the wall had a long production behind it. The room around it had its own work: preparing food, bringing it out, making space for people to arrive and talk. My recordings move between those things, then catch a laughing encounter indoors. The photographs keep the faces and the motion around them.

For this field film, I asked for datamosh: let an image leave something behind as another image arrives. The commissioned animation passes into the courtyard footage, briefly carrying its motion into a different surface. Then the picture clears. The source film and my recording stay identifiable, and the filmmakers keep their credit.

At the KERNEL premiere, I filmed the kitchen and the projection reflected in the wet courtyard.

Now I'm letting the animation leave a little memory inside the footage. Datamosh as an encounter between the film and the room that watched it.

What we choose to spend time on.

Eight months, a studio and a score of its own. This campaign was given people and time. KERNEL chose to commission that work while building tools intended to make other work easier to hand over.

Catherine's writing about the agent web returns to consent and delegation: who gets to act, for whom, and with what permission. Her proposed permissions should be specific, traceable and revocable. That is a useful question to carry alongside a film about people and their agents. Access to a browser is one part of the job. Deciding what to ask for, what to keep and when something is finished remains a set of choices.

I build with AI too. Some images in this essay are AI-edited interpretations of my supplied photographs, made using saved creator prompts and ImageGen. The outfit editorial uses my original face and full-body recording as references. The originals remain in the source archive. That is a different process from Parallel Studios' commissioned animation, and I want the distinction visible.

The last photograph is quieter: two faces together, a name tag still in the picture. A small record after a night built around a much longer piece of work.
