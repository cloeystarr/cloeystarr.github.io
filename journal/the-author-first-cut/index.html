We recorded the voices ourselves.

The first ompom.ai film put our own performances inside an imagined world. The work was getting the voice, image and sound to belong to the same scene.

Words & work by Chloe Starr · Retrospective written October 21, 2026

Chris and I recorded our own dialogue. That is one of the things I want people to know when they watch The Author.

The film brings those recordings into a world made with reference images and animation. It would be easy to describe it by naming the tools. OpenAI for images. Google Flow for motion. Codex, Python and FFmpeg for assembly and finishing. But the interesting part of making it was what happened between those stages.

A voice needs somewhere to land

Our performances stayed our performances. Starlet, the fictional character, had a separately generated Kokoro voice. Those are different sources, and the distinction belongs in the credits. Combining them meant working on timing, captions, the balance of voices and music, and the transitions between scenes.

There were repeated revisions to picture and sound. A change of image could expose a continuity problem. An abrupt musical transition could make the whole sequence feel unfinished. Getting an export out was one milestone. Getting the pieces to feel connected was another.

A first version, and a film to return to

The film reached the website alongside the early version of my personal site. It gave ompom.ai a story people could watch while the company website was still coming together.

I’m returning to it now because I want another cut. That doesn’t erase the work in this one. It gives me something specific to revise: performances we recorded, images we chose, and a sequence that already exists.

Listen to the people inside the picture

The character image can make this look like a project that began with a visual style. The performances are another starting point. We recorded our own dialogue, and the edit had to give those voices time to arrive inside an animated scene. The production credits distinguish our recordings from Starlet’s separately generated voice.

That difference changes what I need to preserve in a recut. I can replace a picture without treating the performance as disposable. I can change the timing without pretending a new image automatically improves the scene. The original release is a version to work from, with a sound and picture sequence I can examine.

The tools have different jobs: reference imagery, motion generation, voice, assembly and sound. The article’s film player keeps the whole sequence available, so the process can be judged against what is actually on screen.
