The town square, in towels

At Afterglow’s peptide party at Alchemy Springs, we talked about Twitter, social hierarchy, and what being known online can make possible.

Chloe Starr · November 7, 2026 · Event: November 6

We were standing around in our swimsuits and towels, talking about the social hierarchy of Twitter.

Twitter is SF’s Times Square. The tech community is there, and its social hierarchy has real consequences. We talked about it in towels at @afterglow_sci + @secretsoupco’s party at Alchemy Springs with @amypretzel @creatine_cycle @lordspline @TheKanter.

Chloe Starr on X · November 7, 2026

That was the substance of our conversation on Friday at Alchemy Springs. Amy, @amypretzel, told us she had raised money for her previous startup through Twitter. It was also how she got her current job. That gives the question of online status some weight. When the people who might fund your work or hire you are on the same platform, being visible there can have very material consequences.

The conversation was funny, too. The idea that being on X makes you a superior human met a reply that it might actually make you worse. Then we were talking about why followers should matter at all, whether someone should judge you by a number, and how San Francisco’s Twitter following compares with LA’s Instagram following. The comparison kept going, into an OnlyFans aside. This was a conversation among people who enjoy being online enough to make fun of it together.

The party was thrown by Afterglow, the peptide company, @afterglow_sci, with Soup. Alchemy Springs was the venue. Afterglow called the event Experiment 02: Body Work. Their invitation proposed a world where thermal spas become the new nightclub, with saunas among friends taking the place of beers at a bar.

There was a real company behind the cultural experiment. Afterglow’s products include dissolving strips and gummies; it describes its approach as making wellness products easier to use. Friday put that company into a social setting: an elixir bar, custom peptide blends on the invitation, food, music, heat and cold, people getting to know each other.

I loved how small and relaxing the gathering felt. I had on a black swimsuit and a yellow towel, my hair piled up. In the garden, people stood talking beneath string lights, plants around them and food laid out on a table. It was quiet and chill enough to stay with a conversation.

The host’s own photos catch another side of the same evening: the Afterglow display and the listening space. They sit beside our footage as another view of the party. Afterglow, @afterglow_sci, and Soup, @secretsoupco, had made a place where a conversation about the internet could wander for a while.

Alchemy gave the evening several places to go. There were sofas and paintings, a little tent in the lounge, a DJ at the decks with records behind them. A doorway glowed purple, with red light beyond it. The invitation offered the sauna and cold plunge, a non-alcoholic elixir bar and a listening lounge. Five to seven was for relaxing and charcuterie; seven to nine promised a change in mood.

The venue has its own social ambition. Alchemy’s vision places bathing and time with friends at the center of an evening out. It is a physical place for the kinds of encounters that an online community can set in motion. You can know someone’s handle before you have ever stood beside them in a garden.

With Amy, Atlas, @creatine_cycle, and Nalin, @lordspline, the conversation moved through the power of the platform and the hierarchy that forms around it. Follower counts came up, along with the comparison between San Francisco’s Twitter following and LA’s Instagram following. The platform where your community gathers becomes part of how that community sees you.

David Kanter, @TheKanter, was there too. He co-founded MLCommons and now leads MLPerf, its machine-learning benchmark effort.

Nalin co-founded Capy, which builds cloud coding agents. His feed gives the town-square idea a very concrete next address. In his November 4 post, he invites builders to the Consumer Mobile Hackathon at the YC office on November 15, presented by Capy, Revyl, Convex and Candle. Applications are due November 13. Here is someone from our conversation using the platform to bring another room together, this time around making mobile apps.

There is a hierarchy of attention: whose post travels, whose opinion gets repeated, who people recognize. Amy’s experience gives that hierarchy a practical dimension. A connection made through a post can become a funding conversation or a job. For someone building something, the audience may include the people who can help it exist.

So much of the tech community is there that the feed can become a public meeting place and an informal introduction system at once. A person can encounter your work before meeting you. They can also encounter an argument about you first.

The house-party dispute was part of our conversation too: someone not being let in, and the drama playing out on X. In his original post, Atlas questioned people arriving at a home without an invitation. His follow-up described capacity limits and more requests than he could answer. One phrase in the original stays with the larger conversation: “meet everyone and know no one.”

A public platform makes people familiar to one another in strange degrees. You might know someone’s jokes, their work, their arguments, the people they reply to. What does that familiarity mean when you arrive at a door? The party argument made the boundary visible. A city can share an online square; a home still has a host.

I thought Atlas was really cool. He likes posting. I just like posting too.

My dad was in computer engineering, and I’ve always had a computer. I’ve basically always been online. I enjoy the community of it, the conversations, the jokes, the people you keep coming across. I’ve posted nonsense for fun. I still like trolling people on Reddit sometimes.

I’ve had different followings through different stages of social media. But I just like posting. I like making vlogs. I like having my ideas out there. My blog doesn’t even have likes. I put something there, then share it across the other places I use on the internet. The pleasure starts with having something I want to put into the world.

That was something I recognized in Atlas. We could talk about the hierarchy and the opportunities, and I could still like him for enjoying the thing itself.

I think what’s coming is a post-like internet. Follower counts will still matter for some things. There is a hierarchy, and moving through it can open doors into different parts of society. Amy’s story is one example of how online connections can change what becomes possible.

But posting also gives your ideas somewhere to be found. Someone who thinks along similar lines can come across something you wrote and recognize a person they want to talk to. That matters to me even when there is no number underneath it.

I just like posting. And I want to find the people who understand what I’m putting out there. If I never start sharing it, how are we going to find each other?

Amy showed me something else she was making, sfrats.com, a map of free and free-ish events around the Bay Area. Music, workshops, markets, comedy. I loved meeting her and seeing an actual project I could explore after the conversation.

Her About page traces the idea back to a free-food club she ran at Stanford, finding campus events with leftover catering. Maddie helps run SF RATS too. It is a practical way of making more of a place available: something is happening, here is where it is, you might want to go.

And the project has already made the trip from website to physical gathering place. In a July 19 post, Amy shows SF RATS events on a tall screen at The Garden, beneath its green neon sign. The list of things to do has become something you can encounter while you are already out in the city. I like that loop: finding a place, arriving, discovering somewhere else to go.

Twitter had helped Amy find funding and work. Now she was showing me a project that could help people find somewhere to go. Afterglow had brought us to Alchemy Springs, where that conversation could happen in swimsuits and towels, with time to listen.

I loved how small and relaxing it was. I liked meeting Amy. I liked talking to Atlas. And I came away with things I wanted to share: her website, the conversation, this little piece of an evening. That is why I keep posting.
