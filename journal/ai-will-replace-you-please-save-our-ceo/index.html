AI will replace you.
Please save our CEO.

Jensen’s exit has become an AI meme festival and a founder rescue campaign. The employee bargain is hiding in the replies.

Eight days after I went to BoxWorks to hear Jensen Huang explain the AI future, Elon Musk was posting about a petition to bring him back to Nvidia. In between: an admission of infidelity, an investigation, a resignation, and an internet that had already cast Huang in The Matrix.

The AI future has acquired a very human succession problem.

The Matrix parody from @Arminn_Ai had about 7.7 million displayed views when I checked it on November 13. Thomas Unise had turned Huang into the star of a synthetic music-video confession. The people selling us tools to remake work are being remade into entertainment by the same kind of tools. Nobody has to wait for a studio, a corporate statement or the subject’s permission to take over his image.

That is funny. It is also a much better introduction to the AI economy than another slide about productivity. Intelligence is getting easier to reproduce. Authority is still attached to particular people. And the internet is remarkably good at taking a man everyone calls irreplaceable and putting his face on somebody else.

THE NEWS / WHAT IS ESTABLISHED

November 8: Huang publicly admits infidelity. Nvidia announces an investigation into reporting of a foreign intelligence effort. At that date the board says it has no reason to believe national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised.

November 12: Nvidia announces his resignation as CEO, president and director, effective immediately; CFO Colette Kress becomes interim CEO.

Keep separate: an admission of infidelity, an investigation, and memes about espionage are different kinds of information. A resignation does not establish every circulating allegation.

Nvidia’s announcement · Huang’s statement · Board statement

Everyone is replaceable. Please save this one person.

Tim Sweeney calls Huang’s departure a sad day for Nvidia, developers, America and humanity. His defence has substance: Huang built a company that kept creating useful technology at enormous scale, rather than simply extracting more money from a captured market. Removing a builder can damage the thing he built. I take that argument seriously.

But watch how quickly the conversation about AI changes when the potentially irreplaceable person is at the top. For workers, the question is how much of the job a model can do. For the founder, the question becomes how much the world might lose without him. Both questions can be legitimate. I want to know why the second kind of imagination is so much easier to find for the person already holding the most power.

The walkout has a vesting schedule

The most revealing exchange I found was smaller than the movie memes. Yun-Ta Tsai urged Nvidia employees who wanted Huang back to “resign and chill,” arguing that hard-to-replace GPU engineers could take their destiny into their own hands. Musk replied that he was signing a reinstatement petition.

Then @0_______J supplied the missing compensation analysis: “Hard to resign and chill with that much unvested NVDA.”

There is the employee bargain, in one reply. You can be valuable enough to have leverage and financially tied enough to the company that using it hurts. The equity that makes a job attractive also raises the cost of leaving it. A call for loyalty can ask ordinary employees to wager compensation on a person whose departure is being treated as a national loss.

My dad worked in tech my whole life and strongly preferred the FAANGs. Established names meant safety. He looked down on startups. I understand that advice much better than I agree with the way it still frames the choice. A liquid stock grant can be an excellent reason to take a job. It cannot tell you whether that company will need you next year, or what disagreeing with it will cost.

Meta’s September-quarter results make the distinction hard to avoid: headcount down 15 percent year over year, alongside $34.91 billion in capital expenditure, including finance-lease principal. The company described infrastructure spending on servers, data centers and networks. This is not a receipt showing each laid-off worker paid for a GPU. It is a successful business expanding its capacity while employing fewer people. 1

The company can afford the future. Your place in it is a separate budget decision.

What the keynote asks you to give away

At BoxWorks, Huang discussed using agents to reproduce business processes reliably. That is a real engineering and product opportunity. Aaron Levie’s more expansive case is that agents will unlock work companies cannot currently get to. I want that possibility: more things we can make, rather than a fixed pile of tasks divided among fewer people. 2

But consider the employee’s contribution. You explain the process, the exceptions, the shortcuts, the knowledge that makes the organization function. You help turn what you know into something the organization can use without you. Keeping the company competitive is a reason for the company to want that. What is the offer to the person doing it?

An AI-native startup can begin with agents inside the work. An incumbent has to renegotiate positions, budgets and authority that already exist. That makes buying models easier than changing who decides. The management layer that wants every worker’s knowledge made explicit also needs to explain what judgment it contributes, and let people examine that explanation.

I am interested in startups because participation can mean more ownership and more influence over what gets made. If the established employer also carries risk, taking a different risk for a different kind of participation deserves a serious hearing. The logo cannot settle it.

A founder is not a transparency policy

Joanne Chen’s joke about every San Francisco founder deciding he is worth spying on is a useful interruption. A smaller company can concentrate power even more tightly. The founder can be the entire approval chain, and the person you would need to complain about.

I worked at Unity. Its former executive Anne Evans alleged sexual harassment by then-CEO John Riccitiello in 2019; Unity denied unlawful conduct and disclosed that the case was compelled into arbitration. I am not claiming I witnessed that alleged conduct. I am asking what makes an institution safe to challenge. 3

Huang’s admission of infidelity is not an admission of sexual abuse. The cases do not become interchangeable because the internet calls everything a scandal. What connects my questions is the protection around people considered essential, and whether someone with less power can demand an answer.

Startups do not get to declare that problem obsolete. The disputed allegations around Delve concern the integrity of compliance evidence itself. A business selling assurance can still face questions about what its own story proves. The useful dividing line is whether claims can be checked and power can be challenged. Company age does not answer either question. 4

Your name needs somewhere else to live

This is why my bet is that everybody needs to become their own storyteller, their own personal brand. I want people to have work, relationships and a story that remain recognizable outside an employer’s name. That is part of why I am building ompom.ai.

The meme economy makes the urgency obvious. A company can spend years building a public image around one person. Then strangers can recast him overnight. Having a personal brand does not mean controlling what everybody says about you. It means having a body of work people can return to when the feed decides who you are this week.

For an employee, that could be a project somebody can examine, an account of a problem you solved, an idea you are still working through. It does not require publishing confidential work or performing your entire life. It means an employer is no longer the only source that can explain why you matter.

And it leaves me with a contradiction. If personal branding becomes a way to make someone impossible to question, I have recreated the founder problem at a smaller scale. The story needs evidence, credit and corrections. Otherwise AI just helps us all write better excuses.

Could it also help us trace a claim, inspect a decision, find the gap between what an organization says and what it did? I hope so. But a system that can examine every employee while keeping executive decisions inaccessible gives management more visibility without making management more accountable.

That is the future I want the keynote to get to: what people can build, own, show and question. Right now we have a very efficient system for turning the founder into a movie. I would like us to get as inventive about giving everyone else a future beyond the company.
