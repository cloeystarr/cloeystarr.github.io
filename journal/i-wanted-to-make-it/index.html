SyncSketch → Unity

Know the work
inside the studio.

ANCIENT WITCHBefore you were making these AI-assisted films, you were selling software to creative teams. What did you need to understand about them?
CHLOE STARR

At SyncSketch, I was talking with people making films, animation and games. The production mattered. A film team might need a tool for three months. If I reached them near the end of a show, I needed to think about the next production. With games, I needed to start the conversation earlier.

The question was where review fitted into the work they were already doing. Who needed to see something? How did feedback move? When could a team try another way?

ANCIENT WITCHYou’ve named Disney, Netflix Animation, Epic Games, Naughty Dog, Skydance and Universal Parks in your account history. Pick apart the work for me. How did you get inside an account before anyone was asking you to sell them something?
CHLOE STARR

The original messages show me looking for the person responsible for review, not just somebody with a senior title. At Naughty Dog, I wrote to several people around the pipeline. At Skydance, I was talking with an animator who already knew the product.

The accounts did not all begin in the same place. Somebody could love using SyncSketch with students and friends without the studio being a customer. I needed to find out which conversation I was actually in.

ANCIENT WITCHYou described designing your own way into Naughty Dog. What does the original outreach show?
CHLOE STARR

In May 2021, I wrote looking for the person who managed the review pipeline. I named the other people I had contacted and included a short introductory video. I was asking for the owner of a particular process, rather than sending an introduction to the studio and hoping it found someone.

In my later outreach to mudstack, I used that as an example of getting into a studio without an existing connection. It was work I could bring into another company serving a related buyer.

ANCIENT WITCHStay with Naughty Dog. You didn’t have a warm introduction. What did you actually send, and what were you asking the person to do?
CHLOE STARR

I wrote to Almudena Soria and other people around the studio, naming Aaron Limonick, Matt Neapolitan and Paul Kovar among the people I was contacting. I asked who managed the review pipeline and linked a short introduction to the product.

The question at the end was specific: could they help me find the right person, or, after seeing the video, was that person them? I gave them a way to route the conversation. I was not asking an artist to become the buyer just because I could reach them.

ANCIENT WITCHFinding the right person is the beginning. At Naughty Dog, what nearly killed the deal after the artists wanted it?
CHLOE STARR

Legal and procurement. The people who would use SyncSketch wanted it for the next production. They could see what it would change in their creative pipeline. But wanting the tool was not the same as being able to buy it.

We were bootstrapped, including the legal support. The contract work was slow, and they were considering Frame.io. The people I had built relationships with told me what was happening: the other vendor was getting back to them on legal, and we needed our lawyer to respond or they would not get to use us.

ANCIENT WITCHSo the relationship did more than get you a meeting. What did it let you see?
CHLOE STARR

That the deal was at risk even though the users still wanted us. Without those conversations, I could have mistaken their enthusiasm for progress.

I was working with both the people who would use the tool and the people who could approve it. The creative team gave me a view of what was happening inside the account. The next obstacle was the contract, not another demonstration.

ANCIENT WITCHUniversal Creative was another long one. Why couldn’t you just close it when the creative team said yes?
CHLOE STARR

They were working with intellectual property for the parks, including visual effects for the attractions. They wanted to use SyncSketch to review that work. But the security requirements were serious. You cannot have that material leak.

When I started, SyncSketch did not have SOC 2. There were penetration-testing requirements too. I could not talk someone out of needing those things. I had to maintain the relationship and return as the product’s security position improved.

ANCIENT WITCHWhat were you doing during the wait, beyond sending another “just checking in” email?
CHLOE STARR

Staying in touch with my contact and being interested in the work itself. I love Harry Potter. The enthusiasm was real. I wanted to understand what they were making, and I kept coming back when we had something relevant to bring to the conversation.

In my recorded account, getting that deal through took more than a year. A good conversation did not make the security requirements disappear. The work was keeping the relationship alive while we became a product they could actually approve.

ANCIENT WITCHGive me a deal where understanding the creative work changed what you went back to the product team to ask for.
CHLOE STARR

Raised by Wolves. I loved the subject matter. The team asked whether SyncSketch could handle dailies as well as VFX review. If it could, they wanted to use us.

So I went back to the CTO: could we do the dailies? We did not already have a customer I could point to using it that way. We had to check what was technically possible. The production went on to use it for dailies.

ANCIENT WITCHAnd what did you see in that conversation besides one sale?
CHLOE STARR

A case study. I was already thinking about how the next customer could understand the work. There was a real question, a technical check, and then a production using the tool.

I like selling with that in mind. What are they trying to make? What do we have to figure out together? And if it works, how do we tell that story so somebody else can recognize their own problem in it?

ANCIENT WITCHWhat about the person who says, “We already have something, and we are not changing it”? Show me how you handled that.
CHLOE STARR

Jon Cowley at FuseFX said exactly why changing tools was difficult. Shotgun was deeply integrated, including with the business and financial side. He could not see them changing it.

I answered with how SyncSketch connected to Shotgun. I explained reviewing the files through SyncSketch, inviting people with a link, adding material during dailies and collaborating across studios. I linked the Sunrise Productions customer story and mentioned upcoming Epic Games and Frontier Studios case studies.

I was showing where the review experience could fit without asking him to pull apart the production system. That was the objection in his message, so that was what I needed to answer.

ANCIENT WITCHAnd Skydance? Before the 125-to-250-seat expansion you describe, what did the first conversations look like?
CHLOE STARR

In September 2020, Sergio Díez del Nozal told me he loved SyncSketch and used it with students and friends. I told him I had tried reaching Skydance and had not heard back.

That exchange shows how early the work started. Personal use gave me something to talk about, but it did not tell me who owned the studio’s review process or who could approve a purchase. My later career account describes the seat expansion. Those are separate stages in the relationship.

ANCIENT WITCHAnd once you had someone’s attention, what did you find yourself wanting to build?
CHLOE STARR

More useful material for the conversation. In June 2021, I was talking with Taran Matharu, who used SyncSketch and had pointed me toward people at MPC. He explained the security protocols and the review tools they already had.

I thought the presentation was holding us back. Some people saw SyncSketch as a product for schools or smaller studios. We had an introductory video people had already seen. I wanted demonstrations and creator content that could help larger teams understand how it might fit.

I watched Taran’s YouTube work, asked how far ahead he planned his topics, and explored whether an Enterprise package could support him. I wanted to bring Bernhard into the conversation and find a partnership that was useful to both sides.

ANCIENT WITCHThen SyncSketch became part of Unity. Why keep that as one chapter?
CHLOE STARR

Because it was the same product and the relationships around it continuing inside a larger company. Unity announced the acquisition in November 2021. It makes more sense to follow the work through that change than to treat the names as unrelated jobs.

The commercial results belong in this chapter too: my career records describe $600K+ in SyncSketch revenue and expanding Skydance from 125 to 250 seats at Unity. One describes revenue; the other describes growing an existing relationship.

Now that I am making films and building agent-assisted workflows myself, I have another perspective on those conversations. I can be the person trying a tool, finding where it fails the work, and asking what needs to change.

2026 / Working with agents

ANCIENT WITCHYou spent years helping people adopt software. What happened when you started building with agents yourself?
CHLOE STARR

In February, I was sending people a tool called Pulse and asking for blunt feedback. The question behind it came straight out of sales: given one goal, which accounts should I focus on right now?

By March, Paul and I were trading messages about Claude and workflows. He asked how to put something into Claude. I wrote, “this is something we both have to learn together.” A few days later, I was telling him you could build n8n workflows with Claude. I had found n8n confusing.

That is a better place to begin this year than pretending I arrived knowing how to do all of it. I had work I wanted to make, and I was learning the tools while trying to make it.

ANCIENT WITCHWhat can someone actually open now?
CHLOE STARR

The website. The films. The archive search. There are poems and stories I wrote years ago beside work I am making now. With Codex, I have been building ways to find the originals and bring selected material into something someone else can visit.

The local recall system searches writing, files, existing media descriptions and exported conversations. I can return to a source: the email, the photograph, the earlier version. The material can become part of the next piece of work.

I direct the work, bring the material, make choices and review what the agents produce. Agent-assisted implementation is part of how these things get built. The decisions are still mine to make.

ANCIENT WITCHWhat have you learned to ask of an agent that you didn’t know to ask at the beginning?
CHLOE STARR

An export can finish and the film can still be wrong. A picture can resemble the narration without showing the action I asked for. A voice can be intelligible and still not sound like me.

The production records got more specific. What is there at the start of a shot? What happens? What changes? Is the person still recognizable? In the sound work, we kept original performances and separate tracks so a picture edit did not have to break a word or a breath.

I have also asked for corrections to be carried forward. My voice, my references, which photograph to use, what I already rejected. I want to spend the next session making the next thing, rather than explaining the same thing again.

The work now / Making technology understandable

ANCIENT WITCHYou’ve moved between very different kinds of technology. How do you learn enough to sell something you didn’t already know?
CHLOE STARR

I start with customer conversations and the existing case studies. I ask the people who know the product the questions a buyer is going to ask me. In an interview this year, I described it as building a cheat sheet I could defend.

At SyncSketch, that meant understanding how a production was put together, who was reviewing the work, and what could stop a studio from approving the tool. With agents, I am also learning by trying to build the things I want to use. I get to find out where my explanation was clear and where it was not.

ANCIENT WITCHWhat do you want to bring to a team building technology that most people are still trying to understand?
CHLOE STARR

At SyncSketch, I needed to understand the production before I could explain where the software belonged. At Legwork, I wanted the next rep to understand the customer well enough to shape the demo around them. At RangeMe, I gave someone a story and examples he could carry into another conversation.

This year, learning to work with agents has put me back in the position of the person figuring it out. I have had to describe what I want, try what comes back, notice what is missing, and find a clearer way to ask.

I want to translate complex technology into everyday language. The questions I care about are the ones someone asks when they are actually trying to use it: What can I make with this? Where does it fit into my work? What should I trust, and what do I still need to check?

A film, a demonstration, a conversation or a community can give someone a way to begin. I want to help make that first useful encounter, then stay close enough to hear what happens next.
