Nooks / Exploratorium / November 4, 2026

On target.

A sales launch at a science museum. A job I know well. A future that doesn’t stop at a better quota.

Chloe Starr

At the Exploratorium, I gave the museum another name.

Ancient Witch.

“I wish my name was ancient witch because My soul is everlasting.” There it was under my photograph. My agent’s name, my face, and an answer that was a little more personal than a job title.

This was a Nooks launch party. Node was the agent being celebrated, and the room had a DJ, magic tricks, and exhibits we could actually play with. We danced. The company had announced $500 million in growth capital, and the founder was talking about all the things that came before this version of the business.

The demo was about sales stress. Very familiar territory.

A number is not a market.

Outside sales, promo demos in Whole Foods during college, account executive, founding AE. There are a lot of versions of that job in my life. I’ve built pipeline myself. Being called an outlier AE never made the systems around the work feel particularly sensible.

There’s a version of sales where the job is to understand who can buy something and why it might matter to them. Then there’s the machinery around it: the activity targets, the conferences, the assumption that sending someone to shake enough hands will somehow produce the number.

There are only so many people who can buy your product. A target doesn’t make the market bigger.

A recent LinkedIn exchange made this very concrete. Yue Xu, an inference-platform founder, contacted me after Builder Odyssey. He’d read the idea about an internet that remembers authorship, introduced Infron, and asked about the model family behind memory and provenance.

The product looked interesting. That didn’t make me a customer for it right now. The message felt like a pitch before the fit had been checked. When I said so, he acknowledged it.

My offer to help was real. Go-to-market, retail, outside sales, building pipeline as a founding AE. This is work I know. When a pitch misses the fit, there’s something specific to fix. Maybe I become a customer later. Why spend that future goodwill on a pitch that doesn’t fit today?

An event guest list tells you who was in a room. An ideal customer profile tells you which accounts the product can serve. A buying window tells you which of those accounts have a reason and the ability to act now. Those are three different populations.

Buying readiness · A practical sales framework

A fit isn’t a buying window.

Chet Holmes’ buyer’s pyramid puts 3% in “buying now” and 7% open to buying. It’s a useful starting point for thinking about the job: most people in a market are not waiting for a sales conversation today.

LinkedIn’s B2B Institute and Ehrenberg-Bass make a related argument through purchase cycles: most potential buyers are between purchases. Being a future customer and being an opportunity this quarter are different things.

These are market-readiness frameworks, not measurements of Infron or Nooks. The exact share changes with the category and buying window.

Chet Holmes’ sales framework: 3% buying now, 7% open to buying. Not a universal measurement, but a useful reminder. An entire market is not an active pipeline. Someone can like your product without needing a sales conversation today.

This is why expertise matters in AI. Knowing what to automate starts with knowing which work is useful. Sales has a lot of activity theater: dead-end conversations, meetings without a buying reason, effort recorded as progress. Making that faster doesn’t make it valuable.

AI could make sales more mathematical. Which accounts can this product serve? What evidence says they need it now? A contract ending, a budget, a live evaluation. An agent that can explain why this company, why this product, and why now would be useful. A hiring announcement or a click is a clue. It isn’t an order.

For a defined quarter, estimate each account’s probability of buying this product, given its fit and the evidence available. Add those probabilities across distinct accounts to estimate expected wins. Then check whether accounts assigned a similar probability actually buy at that rate. The estimate has to earn its confidence.

AI can make sales more mathematical. Define the market. Estimate who can buy, why now, and the probability of a win. Check those predictions against actual purchases. Sending a thousand messages is an activity count. It isn’t a forecast.

The Brex offer was different. After the PressClub event, an email said I was approved for a $500 gift-card offer and described the banking and corporate-card setup as quick and free. It offered to send details. The conditions weren’t in that first message.

So I asked what the conditions were. Then asked for clear bullet points for my agent to evaluate. Michael Berelowitz supplied them: apply for a new account, get approved for the card, spend on it, make a payment that clears, and keep the account in good standing. Banking alone didn’t qualify. The gift was generally due by February 2, 2027.

“No thanks! Cheers!” was the answer. The point isn’t that a promotion can’t have conditions. Put them beside the attractive number, before somebody has to ask twice. To me, leaving that work to the recipient is misleading. A gift-card hook does not establish a need for a corporate card.

@brexHQ emailed that I was approved for a $500 gift-card offer. Asked for the conditions. It required card approval, spending, a cleared payment and an account kept in good standing. Put that beside the $500 in the first email. Attention is not informed interest.

That discomfort had already started in the PressClub presentation. I walked out. As I remember it, the advice about getting press came down to cold outreach and sending 200 or 300 emails. My advice would start somewhere else: which journalists are already writing about the problem this company is working on? Read their reporting. Find the connection. Bring them something relevant.

Press outreach starts with journalists whose actual reporting connects to what you’re building. Read their work. Find the fit. Give them a reason to care. A target of 200 or 300 emails tells you how much you sent, not whether there was a story.

There’s a sales fantasy, the Wolf of Wall Street version, where getting somebody’s attention excuses what it took to get it. That’s an idea of selling I’m done with. As a founder, misleading people is not a practice I’m willing to normalize in tech. Explain the offer, establish the fit, and keep the promise. Those are commercial skills, too.

Sales expertise includes knowing when not to pitch. Add value for people who could use the product. Explain the conditions before asking for their time. Keep the promise afterward. Misleading people to capture attention is not a sales skill worth automating.

The agent has a number.

In Nooks’ own walkthrough, a rep gives Node a quarterly goal. It remembers calls, follows a deal, and notices what is slipping. The promise is recognizable if you’ve ever had to keep every conversation, next step, and internal update in your head.

That could be a relief. A follow-up remembered. Context recovered. Less time proving that work happened and more time having a useful conversation.

But a tool that helps someone reach a goal and a system that chooses an honest goal are two different things. More efficient prospecting doesn’t settle who is actually in the market. Better reminders don’t settle whether the product fits.

What feels archaic to me is the management machinery continuing as if the job itself hasn’t changed. A company gets built, then the process of running it can become its own world. Conferences, reporting, hierarchies. I’m interested in what agents can reveal about the assumptions inside that world, not only how much faster they can move through it.

Nooks · Dan Lee · November 4, 2026
“AI will make companies want more salespeople, not fewer.”

The founder’s bet. Mine extends to what happens on the other side of the deal.

I’m building a company now. The future that interests me has agents on both sides, finding relevant services, sorting context, and bringing people into decisions where they’re needed. The question isn’t only what an account executive can delegate. What can the buyer delegate?

That future has some infrastructure already. Google’s Agent Payments Protocol addresses authority and consent when an agent buys on someone’s behalf. It is not evidence that an enterprise negotiation can run itself. The distinction matters. But the internet where each company needs another app already feels small beside the world I want to build.

And then somebody stops you.

On a walk, Jeff Lee, a street canvasser, did a little acting bit about dropping my love of nature. Then he admitted he’d lied. It was a genuinely creative hook. I stopped.

Not to donate. We talked, I added him on LinkedIn, and I passed along people recruiting SDRs. If you can keep approaching strangers in the street and make someone curious, that gumption can travel.

Then there’s this post from Dan Solomon, newly at Nooks, looking for SDRs after the launch. He asks candidates to understand what the team needs and find a channel that breaks through the noise. Here is the human hiring that sits beside the agent announcement.
