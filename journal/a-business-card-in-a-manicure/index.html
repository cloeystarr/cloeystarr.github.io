My business card became a manicure.

Stars, pearls, glitter, then a tiny NFC coil. A manicure history that eventually opened my website.

Words & work by Chloe Starr · Retrospective written October 21, 2026

I made my business card a manicure. But the manicure part had a history before the business card.

My archive has photographs of my nails months before Cyberglam: translucent pink, little stars, flowers, pearls, glitter. I like art manicures. During Tech Week, that interest met a very practical question. Could something I was already wearing take someone to the website I had just built?

A very small place to make a world

In the June photographs, each nail has its own detail. A silver star sits beside small round studs. Another nail has bright yellow and purple flower shapes. The base stays pale and translucent, so the decoration seems to sit above it. Across the whole hand there is a family resemblance, without every finger repeating the same thing.

That is a useful way to look at the rest of what I’m making. My website has writing, films, an archive, and different worlds to enter. I want them to belong together while leaving room for each one to have a character. Looking back at the nails gives that preference a physical form. It was already there in the things I chose to keep pictures of.

The details keep changing

By September, the photographs show another arrangement: a pearl-like pink center, scattered reflective pieces, a densely glittered nail, and dark curving lines over a clear base. The hand becomes a little collection of surfaces. Some details catch the light; others draw a shape.

These pictures make the Cyberglam story more interesting to me. A photograph of the NFC coil explains the device. The earlier photographs explain why putting it in a nail made sense as something I would actually want. Style is part of the experiment. I still want to like looking at the thing after someone has finished demonstrating it.

Then one nail became a link

At Cyberglam at The Muse SF, a tiny NFC chip linked to chloestarr.me. My work, my gallery, and a way to connect during Tech Week could start with a phone held near my nail. The little orange coil is visible in the event photographs. It gives the technology a scale: this is something that can sit among the decoration on a fingertip.

The chip opens a website. It does not run AI or contain the whole site. My film uses synthetic narration, which is a separate production choice. Keeping those two things clear makes the physical experiment easier to understand.

A place for the gesture to go

The website was changing too. Building it from my archive meant choosing what a person should see first, how to move from a poem to a film, and how much explanation the homepage really needed. The nail could open the door. I still had to make the place worth entering.

That is the part of physical technology I want to keep exploring: what it does when it joins something already in my life. Here it joined an art manicure, a gathering, and a new website. The older photographs keep the style in the story. The film shows what happened when the style acquired a function.
