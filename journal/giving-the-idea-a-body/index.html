Giving the idea a body

At Dimensional’s office warming, a controller, a moving robot and the people around it gave me a way into a complicated technology.

Chloe Starr · November 7, 2026 · Event: November 6

I’m dancing beside the disco ball in a leather jacket, a belted white dress, red tights and black ballet flats. The outfit came from my bedtime anime ritual. I like watching a few anime before I go to sleep, and I’d been watching Smoking Behind the Supermarket with You. She had this cool outfit with a leather jacket over a belted white dress, and I decided to do that for the party. I love dressing like anime.

Chris has a controller in his hands, and there is a robot moving in front of him. Behind him, an enormous American flag takes up the warehouse wall. People stand around watching, holding drinks, talking. The lights keep changing color.

This was our second stop of the night. Dimensional had turned its new Dogpatch facility into an office-warming party. Its name stretched and bent across a wall in purple light. There were sofas, a little goal, a ball on the floor. In my footage, a wheeled, four-legged robot moves through the same space where everyone is hanging out.

I want to translate complex AI technology into visual stories people can follow. At @dimensionalos, my starting point was Chris, a controller and a moving robot in the same frame. A concrete action gives me a way into the technology. @chrisstarrai

Chloe Starr on X · November 7, 2026

My first job at a YC company was LivBlends. It was building a machine for making smoothies, and YC’s 2015 founder interview places it in the Summer 2014 batch. Robots have been part of my working life for a long time.

Looking back through my archive, I found emails from 2014 where I was sending the team smoothie recipe ideas. That made me smile. There’s my approach to sales, already: find something useful to add. In this case, something as practical as a recipe.

Found emails from 2014 where I was sending smoothie recipe ideas to LivBlends, my first job at a YC company. Apparently my sales method was already: find something useful to add. I like seeing that thread in my own archive.

My broader focus is translating complex AI technology into compelling visual stories, and explaining it for people who don’t already understand it. Here, I can start with something visible: Chris, the controller and the robot, all in the same scene.

The camera keeps coming back to that relationship. Chris looks down at the controls, then up at the machine. The robot turns. Someone steps through the frame. A person leans toward it. My phone follows the motion, sometimes a little late.

What does “agentic robotics” actually mean? An AI agent is software that can choose steps toward a goal and use tools to carry them out. In robotics, those tools can help a machine find its way or move an object. The physical action still depends on sensors, motors and control software.

Here’s a simple way to separate the pieces:

  • The body: wheels, legs, motors and sensors. These give the robot ways to measure its surroundings and move.
  • The movement controls: software that turns a command into coordinated motor movements. A person can give the command with a controller, as Chris does here.
  • The AI agent: software that can choose what to try next, using the tools the developers have connected to it. What it can do depends on those tools and the particular robot.

Dimensional hosted the party and builds dimOS. It’s an open-source set of software tools for connecting those parts: getting information from sensors, keeping track of space and sending commands to a robot. Developers can inspect and change the code. (Dimensional, dimOS source, event invitation)

I also photographed a QR code on one of the robots. Following it gives the machine a name: Gogo. It leads to Houdini’s own Gogo page, which identifies it as a K9 security robot. That little square opens another part of the story. Someone has to turn a robot that moves into a service another person can actually use.

The company names are easier to follow when each has a job attached:

  • Houmanoids runs robot services. Its Houdini platform helps organize jobs such as patrols and inspections. The company says it partners with Dimensional on autonomy. My QR photo leads to its page for a robot named Gogo. (Houmanoids, Houdini)
  • DEEP Robotics makes robot bodies. Its wheel-and-leg designs can roll on suitable surfaces and use their legs over uneven ground. Its LYNX M20 family is designed for industrial work, including inspections. Another attendee’s photo shows DEEP branding on the wheeled robots at this party; the exact model variant remains unconfirmed. (Manufacturer’s explanation, Paramendra’s original photo)
  • Unitree also makes robot bodies. The humanoid in my footage looks like its G1, but I haven’t confirmed that identification. A humanoid is a robot with a roughly human-shaped body. Its shape alone doesn’t tell us how independently it can act. (Unitree G1)

Those are different businesses: building the machine, connecting its software, and operating it for a customer. Hardware appearing in a photo doesn’t establish that its manufacturer’s team attended.

To see how the software fits together, imagine asking a suitably equipped robot to go to a doorway. This is an explanatory example, not a task I watched it complete at the party:

  • Find out what’s nearby. Cameras supply pictures. Distance sensors measure how far away surfaces are.
  • Work out where to go. Software uses those readings to estimate the robot’s position and a path toward the doorway.
  • Choose and send an action. An AI agent could request a navigation tool. That tool handles more specific movement instructions.
  • Move and check again. The robot’s controls operate its motors. New sensor readings let the navigation system update its path as conditions change.

Dimensional provides tools for parts of this process, including sensor data, mapping, navigation and remote control. Which parts work on a particular machine depends on its hardware and setup. (Sensor tools, navigation interface, remote control)

In my footage, Chris is holding the controller. We can see a person directing a robot. To establish that an AI chose its destination, we’d need evidence of that decision too. A moving robot doesn’t show us who chose the move.

Dimensional’s own research gives me another perspective to put beside the footage.

Dimensional’s Can Agents Manipulate?, by Dan B.W. Choe, Ruthwik Dasyam and Sundhar Vinodh Sangeetha, asks a practical question: does giving an AI agent useful robot tools help it complete a physical task?

  • The jobs: lift a bin, remove a bottle cap and arrange letter blocks.
  • The comparison: agents using dimOS tools versus agents using a more basic robot interface.
  • The scale: 900 runs in simulation, meaning a computer-generated test environment. The physical robot comparison was much smaller: one run per setup for each of two tasks.
  • The finding: the authors report faster first successes with dimOS. The small physical sample leaves substantial uncertainty about how reliably that result carries over to other real-world tasks.

It’s the company’s own study. It helps explain what the team is testing; it doesn’t establish what the party robots were doing. (Original report)

How do you show an AI agent acting in the physical world? Follow the decisions: what the camera sees, what the software locates, what the robot does next. @dimensionalos’s research gives me another view to put beside my party footage.

Chloe Starr on X · November 7, 2026

A second study, Can Jev Nav?, tests agents navigating simulated homes. There’s an important catch: they receive a written description of the environment, including information a real robot might not have seen yet. Finding a route when you already have that information is a different challenge from discovering the room as you go. (Study by Stash Pomichter, Ruthwik Dasyam and Henry Ventura)

Then I look back at my own recording. The room is full of bodies, chairs, changing light, people passing between my camera and the thing I am trying to film. The research represents the room one way. My phone records it another way. Putting those views together can show both the problem the software is solving and the world it has to work inside.

The evening stays human in my phone. There are photos of Chris beneath the flag. Later I turn the camera toward myself, and he leans into the shot. We end up smiling together beneath the warehouse lights.

My footage from @dimensionalos keeps returning to people: Chris with a controller, someone leaning toward a robot, the two of us squeezing into a selfie. The surrounding humans are part of the story. @chrisstarrai

Chloe Starr on X · November 7, 2026

A QR code gave one robot a name. Another person’s photograph helped identify its hardware neighbors. The company research opens up the software behind the motion. Each view adds something my camera alone could not explain.

Then the fire alarms went off. The fire department broke up the party, and the night moved outside. My last recordings have a different kind of flashing light: a fire engine on the street, people leaving, then my red tights and ballet flats as I walk away.

The fire-engine footage closes the film.

That belongs in the story too. Robots, a disco ball, my anime outfit, and a party that ended with the fire department. I came to look at technology that still has so far to go, and I want to explain what I saw without losing the actual night around it.
