The Dresden Dolls, Lily Allen, and still being a fan.

I went to see my favorite high school band in San Francisco while public judgment swirled around Amanda Palmer. Then she sat at the piano and sang Lily Allen.

Words & work by Chloe Starr · Retrospective written October 21, 2026

I loved The Dresden Dolls since high school. Their songs were where I went as a teenager when the world felt overwhelming, theatrical, and raw. When Chris Starr AI and I walked into Great American Music Hall on October 7, I did not know what Amanda Palmer was carrying onto that stage. Then she sat down at the piano and sang Lily Allen.

A song across two lives

The performance at Great American Music Hall was loud, crowded, and physically alive. Brian Viglione was behind the drums in pantomime makeup, pounding out rhythm with theatrical intensity. But the moment that stayed with me was Amanda covering Pussy Palace, from Lily Allen’s album West End Girl.

I had listened to Lily Allen’s record cover to cover. Amanda had previously shared a video of herself at the piano working through that song, and wrote on Patreon about seeing Lily live in Boston while navigating her own divorce. Hearing those lyrics performed live in San Francisco felt immediate and exposed. Two different women, living across different countries and musical worlds, were using song to speak about betrayal, public spectacle, and the difficulty of rebuilding a life.

A permission email from college

Amanda’s willingness to invite people into her process has always had complications. In 2012, nearly 25,000 Kickstarter backers pledged more than a million dollars for her solo album, art book, and tour. When she initially invited volunteer musicians to join her on stage, it sparked widespread public criticism, and she reversed course to ensure all guest performers were paid.

That openness had also touched my own creative life years earlier. When I was in college, I emailed Amanda asking for permission to use a Dresden Dolls song in the trailer for Deconstructing Paisley, an independent project directed by Mike Mazzella where I played Paisley. Amanda answered personally and gave her permission. It was a generous gesture to a young student trying to make something, and it stayed in my memory long after the project wrapped.

What it means to stay a fan

Finishing this field film required several editorial attempts. My earliest rough cut was little more than concert footage stitched to explanatory titles. It lacked emotional stakes. The cut came together only after I recorded my own narration in San Francisco and allowed multiple perspectives to share the screen: attendee recordings, archival footage, attributed public reporting, and Amanda’s unvarnished performance.

Toward the end of the concert, Amanda and Brian closed the set with Girl Anachronism, my favorite song. Hearing Amanda scream those lines while Brian hammered the kit reminded me of what brought me into the room in the first place. Her music expresses anguish, anger, and the resolve to survive. Being a fan does not require looking away from difficult, unresolved realities. It means acknowledging the complexity of the person on stage while honoring the art that helped you survive your own youth.

Further notes, people & sources

The trust her work had built

Amanda Palmer’s relationship with her audience has always been built on direct, radical vulnerability. In 2008, when her label wanted shots of her stomach cut from the Leeds United video, fans answered by posting photographs of their own bellies online in solidarity. In 2019, her song Voicemail for Jill offered intimate, non-judgmental support to anyone going through an abortion. Her work created a rare kind of permission for women to occupy space without apologizing for their bodies or their grief.

That artistic history was why the recent controversy felt so painful for longtime listeners. In 2025, civil lawsuits accused Neil Gaiman of sexual assault and alleged that Amanda Palmer enabled abuse. Both denied wrongdoing. In February 2026, the Associated Press reported that the lawsuits in federal court were dismissed or withdrawn on jurisdictional grounds, leaving the underlying claims unresolved in the public mind. Online, the discourse had quickly collapsed into outrage and condemnation. When an artist builds their entire identity on community and trust, allegations strike at the center of that connection.
