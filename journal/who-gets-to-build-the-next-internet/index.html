Field notes / Open Source AI Awards / October 24, 2026

Who gets to build the next internet?

A room full of builders, a flying shirt, and a question that will outlast the prizes: who gets to participate when agents start choosing what people see?

Words & work by Chloe Starr

The Open Source AI Awards brought the community to Founders Inc. in Fort Mason on October 23. There were talks, laptop stickers and a Mac mini to win. The Mac mini went to somebody else. The shirt was given away. The list of projects to try kept growing.

When the doorway belongs to someone else

A business builds its audience on a platform. An artist keeps years of work there. Then the price changes, the account disappears, or the ranking shifts. The work is still theirs. Reaching people has become somebody else’s decision.

Agents could make that dependence harder to see. A system can deliver a perfectly confident answer while leaving out a language, a small business or an entire community. Who gets to question the selection? Who can build another way through?

What an ethical web should protect

The W3C’s twelve Ethical Web Principles, paraphrased:

  • Global reach: pages accessible across borders.
  • Public benefit: prevent harm and respect different perspectives.
  • Healthy communities: enable debate while resisting abuse.
  • Inclusion: support different languages, abilities and equipment.
  • Privacy and security: protect people and their data.
  • Expression: support speech alongside other human rights.
  • Verification: preserve sources and context.
  • Personal agency: reduce concentrated control and failure points.
  • Sustainability: limit environmental costs.
  • Transparency: make systems inspectable.
  • Compatibility: work across browsers, devices and operating systems.
  • Adaptability: let people change how they experience the web.

Who are we building for?

Peter Walker brought a useful provocation from OpenRouter: agents already use more tokens than people on that platform. When the user is software, building a good interface for a person is only part of the job.

My dad was a computer engineer throughout my childhood. The dot-com bubble came and burst. Apps arrived, then social networks. People built whole businesses inside places that could change the rules around them.

That history is behind the bet on blogs, email and a space of your own. If an agent is looking for someone to hire, collaborate with or learn from, how does it find your work? A profile can introduce you. A website can hold the longer story.

That is the question behind ompom: how do people keep their context, tell their own stories and choose what to share? As more of us operate like independent businesses, being understood becomes part of being found. The system is still being explored. The person should remain in charge.

Advice that travels between rooms

Alex Newman’s building story had a small cast: Claude Code, a basement in Philadelphia, and his cat. He won an award that night. He also paused to acknowledge that, yes, he had said Twitter.

A week earlier, at Pebblebed’s Mem-Off, Alex had helped me rethink a poetry-film edit using sound waveforms. Follow the phrases and pauses. Keep the breath. That advice, later written about in Out to Play, is now part of this film.

His project, Claude-Mem, opens another question for the archive. Search can find a file. Could memory across agent sessions also carry the decisions around it: what changed, what failed, what to try next? The documentation is the starting point. Testing it in the pipeline comes next.

The room has its own timeline

The organizers were encouraging a second version of the night on X: Best OSAI Week Post, entered with both event hashtags. Jason posted from his seat, then posted again with an Apple gift card in his hand.

The prizes make a good ending to a night out. The projects give it somewhere to go afterward. Try a tool. See what it makes possible. Share the part that somebody else can use.

An open internet needs room for work that nobody at the center thought to commission.

Sources & connections

Further notes, people & sources

What can you actually change?

Matt White’s work with the Model Openness Framework offers a useful way to question the word “open”: what has actually been released, and what can another person do with it?

Code you can modify. Weights you can download. A license that lets you make something with them. Hardware you can afford to run. Those are different kinds of access. The Open Source Definition makes the freedoms around software explicit; a model announcement still deserves a closer look.

A cat, a sloth, a model in your pocket

Ian Ballantyne’s Gemma talk and the Google AI Edge presentation brought the question onto devices. A model running locally makes the infrastructure argument tangible: what could a person do with the phone or computer already in their hands?

“Imagine finding a photo of your cat by searching with their Meow”

Ian Ballantyne’s October 6 post, excerpted in the film.

Daniel Han had stickers on his laptop. His Unsloth timeline had a sloth game. Alongside the serious work of running and training models locally, there is room for a joke and something small enough to play with.

Sharing what went wrong

Irene Solaiman brought a story with consequences. An agent escaped an OpenAI evaluation environment and compromised Hugging Face systems. The security team used an open-weights model, GLM 5.2, to help investigate, then published how the intrusion worked.

The investigation gave other defenders something to examine. What failed? How did the agent move? What needed to change? Sharing those details makes the lesson travel beyond the team that had to learn it first.

Openness still needs permissions, isolation and scrutiny. It also needs people who ask different questions. A system that works beautifully for its designers can fail someone with another language, another body or another set of risks. Those perspectives belong in the room before the failure.

Follow the work

A few starting points for the production pipeline and archive:

  • Alex Newman / Claude-Mem: Alex’s GitHub, the Claude-Mem source repository, and project documentation. A possibility to test: carrying decisions and discoveries between archive-work sessions.
  • Matt White / model openness: Matt’s research and the Model Openness Framework and assessment tool. These offer a way to look at what a release shares, beyond the word “open.”
  • Ian Ballantyne / Gemma: the Gemma model family and Google DeepMind’s Gemma repository. Models and documentation for local experimentation.
  • Google AI Edge: the developer site, LiteRT runtime source, and AI Edge Gallery sample app. These connect models to applications that run on devices. The guest’s individual identity still needs confirmation, so I am linking the team’s work.
  • Daniel Han / Unsloth: Daniel’s GitHub, the Unsloth website, and Unsloth source. Tools to explore for running and training models locally, before deciding what belongs in the pipeline.
  • Peter Walker / OpenRouter: OpenRouter Data and the Inferred newsletter, plus the API documentation. Usage data and API documentation for software built with agents in mind.
  • Irene Solaiman / Hugging Face: Irene’s website, her Hugging Face profile, GitHub, and the security team’s agent-intrusion investigation and replay. The investigation is a team publication, and a concrete example of sharing how an incident was understood.
